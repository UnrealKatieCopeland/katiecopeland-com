Litigant's Toolkit

The Neuro-Legal Script: Exact language for requesting accommodations without opening yourself to "unfit" attacks

How to Use This Toolkit

This toolkit provides the exact language you need to:

  • Request accommodations before your hearing
  • Respond if asked about your disability in court
  • Preserve the issue for appeal if accommodations are denied
  • Recognize what opposing counsel cannot legally do

The goal is to name the mechanism, not the character flaw. You are not asking for sympathy. You are invoking a federal right.

Part 1: The Accommodation Request Letter

This letter should be sent as early as possible — ideally 30 days before any hearing, but send it as soon as you know you need it. Address it to the court's ADA Coordinator (if one exists) or to the Court Administrator if not.

Subject Line:

Request for Reasonable Modification Under Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act

Template:

Dear [ADA Coordinator / Court Administrator],

I am writing to request reasonable modifications under Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act, 42 U.S.C. § 12132, for the hearing scheduled on [DATE] in Case No. [CASE NUMBER].

I have been diagnosed with [CONDITION], which affects my [working memory / processing speed / ability to track rapid verbal exchanges / ability to remain upright for extended periods / sensory processing]. As a result, I require the following accommodations to participate meaningfully in the proceeding:

1. [Accommodation 1]: [Brief explanation of why this is necessary — e.g., "Extended time to respond to questions, as my condition causes delays in word retrieval under stress."]

2. [Accommodation 2]: [e.g., "Written summaries of key rulings and instructions, as I cannot reliably retain verbal information delivered at conversational speed."]

3. [Accommodation 3]: [e.g., "Scheduled breaks every 60 minutes, as prolonged upright positioning causes blood pooling and cognitive decline."]

I have attached a letter from my treating physician, [Dr. NAME], confirming my diagnosis and functional limitations. This letter is submitted solely to support this accommodation request and is not offered as evidence in the underlying case.

I am available to participate in an interactive process to discuss these accommodations or identify alternatives that would provide effective communication and meaningful participation.

Please confirm receipt of this request and advise me of the court's response in writing.

Respectfully,

[YOUR NAME]
[DATE]

What to Attach:

A one-page letter from your treating physician (not a full medical history) that:

  • Confirms the diagnosis
  • Describes the functional limitations (e.g., "Patient has difficulty with speeded word retrieval and sustained attention under stress")
  • States that the requested accommodations are medically necessary

Part 2: What to Say in Court (If Asked About Your Disability)

If the judge or opposing counsel asks you about your disability during the hearing, keep your answer short, calm, and mechanism-focused. Do not apologize. Do not over-explain.

Script:

"I have [CONDITION], which affects my [working memory / processing speed / ability to track rapid exchanges]. I requested accommodations in writing on [DATE], and I have not received a response. I am happy to participate fully, but I may need [specific accommodation] to do so effectively."

What NOT to say:

Avoid These Phrases:

  • "I'm sorry, I'm just really struggling today."
  • "I know this is inconvenient."
  • "I'm doing my best."

Why: Those phrases frame your disability as a personal failing or an inconvenience. The accommodation is a legal right, not a favor.

Part 3: Red Flags — What Opposing Counsel Cannot Do

Your accommodation request is an administrative process between you and the court as a public entity. Opposing counsel has no legal standing to interfere with it.

They CANNOT:

  • Move to strike your medical documentation if it was submitted solely in support of an accommodation request (not as evidence in the case).
  • Cross-examine your doctor about your diagnosis if the doctor was offered only to explain functional limitations, not to testify about your fitness as a parent.
  • Demand access to your full medical history as a condition of granting accommodations.
  • Argue that granting accommodations would be "unfair" to them.

If Opposing Counsel Files a Motion to Strike:

You may respond (in writing or orally, depending on the court's procedure) with:

"Your Honor, the medical documentation submitted on [DATE] was filed in support of an administrative accommodation request under Title II of the ADA. It was not offered as evidence in the underlying case. Opposing counsel has no standing to challenge an accommodation request, and their motion to strike interferes with a process protected by the ADA's anti-retaliation provision, 42 U.S.C. § 12203(b)."

Part 4: If Your Accommodations Are Denied

If the court denies your request (or ignores it), you need to make a record so the issue is preserved for appeal.

Before the Hearing Begins, State for the Record:

"Your Honor, I submitted a written request for accommodations under the ADA on [DATE]. I have not received a written response. I am requesting that the court confirm on the record whether my request has been granted or denied, and if denied, the reason why."

If the Court Refuses to Answer or Says the Accommodations Are Unnecessary:

"Your Honor, I respectfully request that the court's ruling on my accommodation request be included in the written order so that I may have a record for appeal."

Why This Matters:

Appellate courts cannot review what is not in the record. If the accommodation denial is never written down, it is functionally invisible. You are creating the paper trail that makes the harm visible.

What You Do NOT Need:

Part 6: Sample Accommodations (Choose What You Actually Need)

For Working Memory / Processing Speed:

  • Extended time to respond to questions
  • Written summaries of key rulings and instructions
  • Permission to refer to written notes during testimony
  • A copy of the hearing agenda in advance

For Sensory Processing / Attention:

  • Scheduled breaks every 60-90 minutes
  • Reduced courtroom lighting (if fluorescent lights cause overload)
  • Limits on cross-talk (one person speaking at a time)

For Physical Limitations:

  • Permission to sit or stand as needed
  • Breaks to allow for recumbency (lying down) if blood pooling occurs
  • Shorter hearing days with more frequent sessions

For Communication:

  • A support person (not an attorney) to assist with organization and note-taking
  • Real-time transcription (CART)
  • Simplified language from the judge (plain English, not legal jargon)

Part 7: The Core Reframe

You are not asking for special treatment. You are not asking the court to lower its standards. You are asking the court to provide the tools that make the standard accessible.

A wheelchair ramp does not give someone an unfair advantage. It allows them to enter the building. A cognitive accommodation does not give you an unfair advantage. It allows you to participate in the proceeding.

The law already says you have this right. This toolkit helps you invoke it.

Part 8: What to Do If You're Already Past the Hearing

If you've already had a hearing where accommodations were denied or ignored, you may still be able to raise this issue on appeal or in a motion for reconsideration.

In a Motion for Reconsideration or New Trial:

"The court's failure to respond to my ADA accommodation request, submitted on [DATE], deprived me of meaningful participation in the proceeding. This constitutes a structural due process violation and a violation of Title II of the ADA. I request a new hearing with appropriate accommodations in place."

On Appeal:

Your appellate brief should argue:

  • The court had an affirmative obligation under Title II to provide accommodations.
  • The failure to provide them rendered the proceeding fundamentally unfair.
  • The trial court's credibility findings are unreliable because they were based on observing an unaccommodated litigant.

This is a structural error argument, not a sufficiency-of-the-evidence argument. You are not asking the appellate court to second-guess the judge's credibility call. You are asking them to find that the process itself was defective.

Additional Resources

If You Need Help Finding an Attorney:

  • Contact your state's Protection & Advocacy agency (every state has one, federally funded)
  • Contact the nearest disability rights legal clinic
  • Search for attorneys who practice both family law AND civil rights / ADA law (they are rare, but they exist)

If You Cannot Afford an Attorney:

  • Many states have self-help centers in the courthouse that can help you file written requests
  • Legal aid organizations may be able to help with accommodation requests even if they cannot represent you in the full case
  • Law school clinics sometimes take ADA court access cases
