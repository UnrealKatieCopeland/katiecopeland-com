Rights Require Routes

Tarrant County Court Access Evidence Hub

A documented, media-facing overview of ADA Title II access-route concerns in Tarrant County courts. The frame is simple: Participation Is the Measure. A right is not meaningful if disabled court users cannot identify the route for requesting, receiving, and reviewing accommodations.

Civic scale

This is not a niche access issue. It is basic courthouse infrastructure.

Tarrant County sits inside one of the fastest-growing civic landscapes in the country. Fort Worth has reported a population of 1,028,117 residents and identifies itself as the 10th-largest city in the United States. The U.S. Census Bureau’s 2024 ACS S1810 disability table estimates 257,817 Tarrant County residents with disabilities. Court access is therefore not a special exception to ordinary government service. It is part of the ordinary design of justice.

Title II Public-entity duty

ADA Title II applies to state and local governments, including public services and programs that determine court access.

The documented access gap

The concern is not whether a courthouse has a promise of access. The concern is whether people can find and use the route.

The materials collected here focus on infrastructure: ADA coordinator identification, grievance procedures, self-evaluation records, accommodation routing, and the practical handoff between county administration and individual courts. The central public question is restrained and concrete: when a disabled court user asks for access, who receives the request, who evaluates it, who communicates the decision, and what review path exists if the route breaks down?

“Participation Is the Measure.”

This page uses participation, not paperwork, as the test. Policies matter because they create the route. If the route is invisible, fragmented, or undocumented, access becomes fragile for the people most likely to need clarity.

Coordinator and grievance infrastructure

Records gathered for this page identify gaps concerning centralized ADA coordinator information, grievance procedures, and the practical process for escalating access concerns.

Self-evaluation records

ADA Title II self-evaluation materials are foundational because they show whether a public entity has examined barriers in its own programs and services.

Accommodation routing

The evidence highlights a recurring problem: disabled court users may be directed between county administration and individual courts without a clearly owned, accountable route.

Digital and physical access

Modern court access includes physical buildings, records systems, websites, communications, hearing logistics, and forms. A fragmented route can become a real barrier.

National civil-rights context

Access to justice is disability access.

The Department of Justice has emphasized that state and local governments must make their services, programs, and activities accessible to people with disabilities. In court systems, that principle is not abstract. It affects custody, housing, benefits, safety, liberty, and family stability. The question is not whether access sounds good in principle. The question is whether the public can use it under pressure.

Reader pacing note: This subject can be heavy. The purpose of this hub is to reduce confusion, not increase alarm: one page, clear documents, and a focused question about whether public rights have usable public routes.

Media and civic inquiry

The public-interest question is narrow, verifiable, and urgent.

In a county serving hundreds of thousands of disabled residents, the justice system should be able to identify the route for requesting accommodations, reviewing denials or breakdowns, and documenting responsibility. That is the heart of this hub. Rights require routes, and participation is the measure.
