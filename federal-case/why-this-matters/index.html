The Open Question

Who is responsible when a courthouse has no disability-access pathway?

No circuit has decided whether § 35.107's administrative obligations — coordinator, intake, grievance — extend to court-participation accommodations. This case presents that question with the defendant's own written admissions.

Five Reasons This Case Is Significant

What Makes This Different

Most ADA cases argue over whether a specific accommodation was reasonable. This case argues something upstream: the infrastructure to process any accommodation request doesn't exist — and the County admits it in writing.

01

Structural, Not Individual

This is not a dispute about one denied request. It challenges the absence of any entity-level ADA process for court users — no coordinator, no grievance procedure, no written pathway of any kind.

Source: Tarrant County Open Records Response, CDA-2026-897
02

The Defendant Admits It in Writing

The County's own open-records response states it was "unable to locate any responsive information" regarding ADA compliance infrastructure. The central facts are not in dispute.

Source: CDA-2026-897, April 21, 2026
03

Narrow, Process-Only Relief

The relief sought is not a ruling on any state-law merits. It is simply a pause on new deadlines until a lawful ADA pathway is identified. This respects federalism while protecting fundamental rights.

Emergency Application, Relief Requested
04

A Federal Judge Said She'd Grant It — Twice

Judge Jennifer Haynes of the Fifth Circuit twice indicated she would grant temporary relief or carry the issue to the merits panel. Her dissent is in the record.

ECF Docs. 26-1/26-2 and 37-1/37-2, No. 26-10389
05

National Precedent Potential

A holding that § 35.107 reaches court-participation accommodations would require every public entity operating a courtroom to maintain what Tarrant County admits it lacks. The courts that enforce the ADA would themselves have to comply with it.

28 C.F.R. § 35.107; Tennessee v. Lane, 541 U.S. 509 (2004)
06

Disability Adjudicated Before the Proceedings

The Social Security Administration ruled the applicant fully disabled — retroactive to June 8, 2023 — before any of the state proceedings at issue. The harm is not speculative.

SSA Administrative Law Judge, Finding 11, April 10, 2026
The Written Record

What the County Said

On April 1, 2026, a senior Tarrant County official was asked how the County processes ADA accommodation requests for court users. The response was recorded.

The open-records response followed on April 21, 2026: "Unable to locate any responsive information" regarding an ADA coordinator, self-evaluation, grievance procedure, interactive process, or written determinations — all five items required under 28 C.F.R. Part 35 since January 26, 1992.

The Stakes

What a Decision Would Mean

  • ⚖
    For disabled court users everywhere A ruling that § 35.107 reaches court-participation accommodations would require every courthouse in the country to maintain a real, accessible process — not just route requests to individual judges with no review.
  • 📋
    For the ADA's administrative framework The case would clarify whether the coordinator, intake, and grievance obligations of 28 C.F.R. § 35.107 apply to court-participation contexts — a question no circuit has squarely decided.
  • 🏛
    For courts as public entities Courts that enforce the ADA against employers, schools, and governments would themselves be required to comply with the same infrastructure obligations they impose on others.
  • ✍
    For the scholarly record The record includes verbatim transcripts, written admissions, and a federally adjudicated disability determination — an unusually clean factual foundation for a structural ADA claim.
