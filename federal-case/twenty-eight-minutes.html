Copeland v. Tarrant County · No. 26-10389 (5th Cir.)

Twenty-eight
minutes.

The whole structural argument, documented on a single Wednesday afternoon — in writing, from both directions, by two different arms of the same county.

Five days earlier

Friday, April 12, 2024, 7:26 p.m. — A request submitted through Tarrant County's official online Public Accommodation Request form. It asked for written submissions in lieu of oral argument, extended time, 10–15 minute breaks, a slower pace, CART, court documents in large print or audio, a support person at counsel table, plain-language explanation of legal terms, and a waiver of record costs — supported by documented cognitive impairment.

2:29:09 pm Wednesday, April 17, 2024
Katie Copeland  ·  Office of Judge David Evans, Local Administrative Judge
"I have emergency motions to present and an application for protective order, but none of the family court judges will look at it. They are claiming that only Judge Wells can rule on our case, but she is out of town for weeks. A court coordinator suggested I ask Judge Evans what to do."
She asked one question: "Can Judge Evans appoint another Judge to hear these motions today or tomorrow?" Under Tex. Gov't Code § 74.092, the Local Administrative Judge is the official responsible for implementing accessibility requirements in the county's courts. No response from that office appears in the record.
28 minutes later
2:57:27 pm Wednesday, April 17, 2024
Tarrant County ADA Office  ·  Katie Copeland
"Good Afternoon Ms. Copeland

Your ADA request would need to go directly through the court your case is assigned to."
That was the entire message. The court her case was assigned to was the one whose judge was out of town for weeks — the fact she had reported to the administrative judge twenty-eight minutes earlier.
6:06:02 pm Wednesday, April 17, 2024
U.S. Department of Justice, Civil Rights Division
Report received. Record number 436059-JVZ.
An automated acknowledgment — not a finding, not an investigation, and not evidence that DOJ agreed with anything. It establishes one fact only: by the end of that same afternoon, she had reported it.

This is the closed loop, with a timestamp

The courts said the county. The county said the courts.

Twenty-eight minutes apart, both in writing, the same afternoon.

No conspiracy is required to produce this, and none is alleged. It is what happens when a public entity builds a Title II process for its buildings and assigns courtroom access to no one. Each office answered correctly, according to its own policy. The request simply had nowhere to land.

On the DOJ entry

The Civil Rights Division's message is an automatic receipt. It states plainly that the Division "may not always be able to provide you with updates" and does "not have the resources to follow-up on every letter." It should never be characterized as a DOJ investigation, finding, or endorsement. Its value is the timestamp.
